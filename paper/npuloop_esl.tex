\markboth{IEEE Embedded Systems Letters}{Per-Operator Attribution of the Fake-Quantization-to-Integer Gap}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
INT8 deployment decisions are made on a simulator. The network runs in floating point with
quantize--dequantize (``fake-quantization'') nodes inserted, and its accuracy stands in for the accuracy
of the integer target. We measure how far that stand-in holds --- for accuracy and for tensors separately
--- against an integer engine that is bit-exact with the TensorFlow Lite reference kernels. On six
networks, two quantization schemes and complete test splits, it holds for accuracy: every
fake-versus-integer difference is within $0.20$~pp and all twelve $95\%$ confidence intervals contain
zero. It fails for tensors: $28.5$--$72.7\%$ of the output codes differ, on $95$--$100\%$ of the images.
Teacher forcing attributes the divergence per operator. Across these networks convolution, linear and pooling
each disagree on under $1\%$ of their own codes, while integer LayerNorm disagrees on $24.8\%$. Making the simulator
integer-faithful at LayerNorm removes that source entirely ($24.77\% \rightarrow 0.00\%$) but moves the
propagated output mismatch only from $72.7\%$ to $65.7\%$: a simulator cannot be repaired into a source of
bit-accurate vectors one operator at a time. We also price a requantization choice the accuracy proxy
cannot see --- replacing rounding with truncation costs $0.90$--$3.37$~pp in all 18 (model, seed, mode)
combinations, and the sign of that cost transfers across seeds while its magnitude does not. The decoupling
survives the removal of the argmax: on a dense-output network the quality metric is faithful to
$6\times10^{-4}$~dB while $6.3\%$ of the delivered pixel codes differ, and tripling that network's operator
count at a fixed width raises the differing fraction to $69.2\%$ with the metric still faithful.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{T}{wo} programs decide an INT8 deployment. The one that answers questions is a
floating-point graph with quantize--dequantize (``fake-quantization'') nodes, whose accuracy is reported as
the quantized accuracy~\cite{jacob2018,krishnamoorthi2018}. The one that ships is an integer program: int8
weights, an int32 accumulator, and a fixed-point multiply-and-shift requantization~\cite{gemmlowp}.
Practice treats the two as interchangeable, and for accuracy they largely are~\cite{nagel2021}.

Accuracy is not all that is asked of the simulator. Silicon bring-up and compiler validation need
\emph{golden vectors}: the exact integer tensors the device must produce. Triaging a post-training
quantization regression needs to know \emph{which} operator started the divergence. Both are tensor
questions, and to our knowledge neither has been measured. We ask three.
\textbf{RQ1:} does agreement on accuracy imply agreement on tensors?
\textbf{RQ2:} which operators create the divergence, and does removing the largest one close it?
\textbf{RQ3:} what does the accuracy proxy fail to price?

Answering them needs a decomposition that current layer-wise error analyses do not provide: separating what
an operator's own integer arithmetic contributes (\emph{local} mismatch, isolated by teacher forcing) from
what survives end-to-end execution (\emph{propagated} mismatch). That decomposition, reported with paired
standard errors on full test splits, is this letter's methodological contribution; Findings~1--6 are its
empirical ones.

\section{Background and Related Work}
A fake-quantization graph rounds and clips a tensor to the quantization grid and then returns to float, so
every operator still runs in float32. The integer program instead keeps int8 codes end to end and rescales
with a Q31 multiplier and a shift~\cite{jacob2018,gemmlowp}. The two therefore agree on \emph{intent} and
are free to disagree on every least-significant bit.

MQBench~\cite{mqbench} measures the gap between academic quantization and deployable backends and reports it as
accuracy; the quantization white papers~\cite{nagel2021,krishnamoorthi2018} treat fake quantization as the
simulation of the integer pipeline; layer-wise error analyses in deployment toolchains quantify how far a
quantized layer is from its \emph{float} counterpart. Ours is a different measurement: how far the
\emph{simulator} is from the \emph{integer execution}, in codes rather than in signal-to-noise ratio.
Integer-only transformer inference has established integer LayerNorm and softmax kernels~\cite{ibert,fqvit};
our contribution there is not the kernel but the size of its disagreement with the simulator. Accumulator width
has been studied as a training-time constraint~\cite{a2q}; the axis here is instead the rounding of the
requantization step, a pure implementation choice.

\section{Measurement Setup}

\subsection{Integer engine and external validation}
A calibrated fake-quantization graph is exported to an integer graph: int8 weights, int32 biases, and a
per-output-channel Q31 multiplier and shift. Execution follows the gemmlowp/TensorFlow Lite semantics of
\texttt{MultiplyByQuantizedMultiplier}~\cite{gemmlowp}. Two independent implementations (NumPy and C++) must
agree on \emph{every} intermediate tensor, and a third, Python-free C++ executor reproduces the same codes
from a serialized file.

We validate the engine against an external oracle, not against itself. Building the integer graph from a
TensorFlow Lite full-integer model's own scales, zero-points, weights and biases, the two agree on all
tensors for $1000/1000$ images when convolution and pooling use the legacy double rounding and the
fully-connected layer uses single rounding. For calibration of how tight that criterion is: TensorFlow Lite's
own optimized delegate and its reference kernels differ on $155/1000$ images of the same model.

\subsection{Local and propagated mismatch}
For one image, let $f_n$ be the codes the fake-quantization graph produces at node $n$ (its quantizer output,
converted to integer codes) and $i_n$ the codes the integer engine produces when run end-to-end. The
\emph{propagated} mismatch at $n$ is the fraction of elements with $f_n \neq i_n$. The \emph{local} mismatch
feeds node $n$'s integer kernel the fake-quantization codes of its inputs (teacher forcing) and compares only
its own output. Local mismatch isolates what an operator's integer arithmetic contributes; propagated
mismatch shows how far that contribution travels.

\subsection{Statistics, models and schemes}
Accuracy differences are paired: the same images are evaluated three times (float, fake-quantization,
integer). We report the mean per-image difference in correctness with its standard error $s/\sqrt{n}$ and a
$95\%$ Wald interval. Accuracy columns use the complete test split ($10{,}000$ images for CIFAR-10, $3{,}925$
for Imagenette); the per-node decomposition uses a fixed batch of $250$ images ($125$ for Imagenette).

Five CIFAR-10 networks --- ResNet-20 with ReLU and with SiLU, MobileNetV2-0.5 (depthwise), a
concatenation-branch Inception-style CNN, and a ViT-128/6 (12 attention blocks, 13 LayerNorms) --- plus two
Imagenette networks at $128\times128$: a ResNet-20 and the super-resolution network of
Section~\ref{sec:dense}. Two post-training schemes, per-channel weights with asymmetric activations
(\textsc{pc}) and per-tensor weights (\textsc{pt}), both calibrated on $512$ training images. Everything runs
on CPU; every number below is emitted by a script from a committed results file.

\section{Results}

\subsection{RQ1: accuracy agrees, tensors do not}
Table~\ref{tab:fidelity} is the main measurement. The largest fake-versus-integer accuracy difference is
$0.20$~pp, the largest $|\Delta|/\mathrm{SE}$ is $1.6$, and all twelve confidence intervals contain zero. At
these sample sizes the accuracy proxy is not distinguishable from exact, and image-level top-1 agreement is
$97.9$--$99.7\%$. The same rows disagree on tensors: between $28.5\%$ (Inception-32) and $72.7\%$
(ViT-128/6) of the output logit codes differ, on $95$--$100\%$ of the images. The transformer is the extreme
case but not a special case --- the CNNs sit at $50$--$63\%$ and the larger-input Imagenette model at $36\%$.

\finding{1} Accuracy agreement and tensor agreement are decoupled by more than two orders of magnitude, on
every network and both schemes.

\implication A reported $99\%$ accuracy agreement is no evidence that the simulator can supply a
verification vector.

\subsection{RQ1 where the tensor is the deliverable}
\label{sec:dense}
Every network above is a classifier, so an argmax stands between the output tensor and the answer. We remove it
with a sub-pixel super-resolution network~\cite{espcn} --- $64 \rightarrow 128$ on Imagenette, $30.23$~dB against
$27.26$~dB for a nearest-neighbour upsampler --- whose output \emph{is} the picture. The pixel shuffle needed no
new operator: it is a reshape and a transpose. To separate depth from task we measure two depths at one width
profile, three convolutions and nine; the deeper one is not the better one ($30.11$~dB), only the same task at
three times the operator count.

Table~\ref{tab:dense} repeats the pattern and puts a number on depth. At three convolutions quantization costs
$0.279$ and $0.667$~dB and the simulator predicts that cost to within $6\times10^{-4}$~dB --- resolvable here
($|\Delta|/\mathrm{SE} = 17.6$), three orders of magnitude below the cost predicted --- yet $6.3\%$ and $18.2\%$
of the output pixel codes differ, on all $3{,}925$ images, by up to three levels. At nine the metric stays faithful
to $3\times10^{-3}$~dB while the differing fraction rises to $69.2\%$ and $73.0\%$ and the largest deviation to
ten levels. The per-operator structure is the CNNs' at both depths: convolution disagrees locally on
$0.06$--$0.40\%$ of its codes, always by $\pm 1$ LSB, first at the first convolution.

\finding{2} The decoupling is not an artifact of classification, and depth rather than task sets the size of the
propagated fraction: nine convolutions on the same images move it from $6.3\%$ to $69.2\%$, into the $28$--$73\%$
band the classifiers occupy, the quality metric faithful throughout.

\implication A simulator can no more supply a reference image than a reference logit vector, and the deeper the
graph the less of the delivered tensor it reproduces.

\subsection{RQ2: where the divergence is born}
Fig.~\ref{fig:decomposition} separates the two components. Local mismatch is small and flat: convolution
disagrees on $0.02$--$0.93\%$ of its codes, linear on $0.03$--$0.96\%$, average pooling on $0.1$--$2.7\%$
(rounding ties), and the int8 lookup-table activations on exactly $0\%$. Every one of these is a $\pm 1$
least-significant-bit disagreement --- as is every local disagreement in the study, LayerNorm's
included. Propagated mismatch instead rises monotonically from the first
convolution --- the first divergence is that node in all twelve rows --- and reaches $28$--$73\%$ at the
output.

One operator is not small. Integer LayerNorm disagrees with its floating-point counterpart on $24.8\%$ of its
codes, with a maximum of $26.2\%$ over the 13 instances. The integer implementation normalizes with an int64
sum and an exact integer square root, while the simulator computes in float32 and then snaps to the grid, so
roughly one element in four lands on the other side of a rounding boundary.

\finding{3} Outside LayerNorm, every operator's own integer arithmetic disagrees on under $1\%$ of its codes,
and every such disagreement is $\pm 1$ LSB. The end-to-end gap is therefore grown by propagation, not
contributed by any one kernel.

\finding{4} Integer LayerNorm is the single large local source, two orders of magnitude above every other
operator.

\implication When porting a transformer to INT8, suspect LayerNorm first and expect $\pm 1$ LSB everywhere
else.

\subsection{RQ2: removing the largest local source}
If the divergence were a sum of per-operator errors, removing the largest would help proportionally. We test
this: the simulator's LayerNorm is replaced by a module that computes the engine's arithmetic on the input
codes, bit-exact by construction, while the exported integer program is unchanged (the experiment asserts so).

Table~\ref{tab:layernorm} shows the outcome. Local LayerNorm mismatch goes from $24.77\%$ to exactly
$0.00\%$, and the mean absolute logit difference improves by about $23\%$. The propagated output-code
mismatch moves only from $72.7\%$ to $65.7\%$ (per-tensor: $72.6\% \rightarrow 66.0\%$). Accuracy moves
within about two paired standard errors in either direction and cannot be read as an improvement.

\finding{5} Eliminating the single largest local source recovers about a tenth of the propagated gap, because
what remains is the amplification of $\pm 1$ LSB events from operators that individually disagree on under
$1\%$ of their codes.

\implication Golden vectors have to come from an integer implementation validated against an external
reference. Repairing a floating-point simulator operator by operator converges too slowly to substitute for
one.

\subsection{RQ3: a cost the accuracy proxy hides}
The requantization multiply-and-shift ends in a rounding step, which an implementation may be tempted to
replace with a plain arithmetic shift. Fig.~\ref{fig:rounding} and Table~\ref{tab:rounding} price that choice
on full test splits, with three independently trained checkpoints per network.

\texttt{truncate} and \texttt{floor} are worse than the reference in all 18 (model, seed, mode) combinations,
by $0.90$--$3.37$~pp, with $|\Delta|/\mathrm{SE}$ between $4.4$ and $11.8$; the alternative single-rounding
and round-half-to-even paths stay within noise ($|\Delta| \leq 0.13$~pp). The magnitude does not transfer. For
ResNet-20 SiLU the cost of \texttt{truncate} ranges from $1.36$ to $2.98$~pp across three seeds, a seed
standard deviation equal to $37\%$ of the effect, while ResNet-20 ReLU stays within a tenth of its effect.
Which biased mode is worse is a property of the network, not of the arithmetic: \texttt{floor} loses more on
all three ResNet-20 ReLU seeds, \texttt{truncate} on all three MobileNetV2 seeds.

\finding{6} The sign and significance of a rounding-mode cost are seed-robust; its magnitude is not, varying
by up to $37\%$ of the effect across three seeds of the same architecture.

\implication Do not quote a single-seed number as the cost of a rounding mode.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\emph{External.} Beyond the CIFAR-10-scale and Imagenette networks we trained, the split holds on a ResNet-50
carrying Keras's ImageNet weights (53 convolutions, $K = 4608$): accuracy within $|\Delta|/\mathrm{SE} \le 0.2$
while $57$--$58\%$ of output codes differ on all $3925$ images --- an ImageNet-scale graph and ImageNet weights,
not the ImageNet-1k validation set. Depth is the one scale axis varied directly (Section~\ref{sec:dense}) and
moves the propagated fraction by an order of magnitude, so that fraction is a property of the graph measured,
not a constant of INT8.

\emph{Construct.} Local mismatch is teacher-forced: it measures an operator's arithmetic in isolation, and the
propagated column is what captures its interaction with upstream error.

\emph{Conclusion.} The sixteen accuracy comparisons in Tables~\ref{tab:fidelity} and~\ref{tab:layernorm} are
not corrected for multiple comparisons; the largest $|\Delta|/\mathrm{SE}$ over them is $2.2$. Three seeds
cover every requantization axis; the rest use one checkpoint per network.

\emph{Internal.} The external bit-exactness check covers convolution, pooling and fully-connected layers, not
depthwise convolution, element-wise addition or softmax.

One caveat applies to the seeded measurement: the trainer's seed controlled data order but not weight
initialization when these checkpoints were produced, so the three replicates are independent draws that the
same command will not reproduce bit for bit.

\section{Conclusion}
Simulated quantization predicts INT8 quality well and INT8 tensors badly, on a classifier and on a network
whose output is the product, and the two facts are independent. The gap is not carried by any single operator:
it is born as $\pm 1$ LSB events in operators that individually disagree on under $1\%$ of their codes and grown
by propagation, which is why removing the worst local source recovers only a tenth of it. Teams that need golden
vectors should generate them from an integer implementation validated against an external reference, and teams
that change a requantization detail should measure it across seeds, because its magnitude --- unlike its sign
--- does not transfer.
